Elżbieta Aleksandrowska's "Geography of the Writers' Community"
======

On January 21, 2026, as part of the ongoing "Digital Environment" seminar of the Institute for Digital Humanities Research (DHRI) at Siberian Federal University, a talk was given by Boris Valentinovich Orekhov, Candidate of Philological Sciences (HSE University, Institute of Russian Literature of the Russian Academy of Sciences, Lomonosov Moscow State University).

The topic of the presentation: "Elżbieta Aleksandrowska's article 'The Geography of the Writers' Community' — a forgotten episode of the spatial turn in the humanities."

The event was devoted to the historiographic reactualization of a significant but undeservedly overlooked work by a Polish researcher, published back in 1977. The speaker presented a retrospective analysis of E. Aleksandrowska's article, which anticipated many ideas of the contemporary "spatial turn" in the humanities by decades.

Key aspects of the talk

- The scale of the study. In Aleksandrowska's work, carried out at the intersection of historical statistics and the geography of literature, data on 533 writers of the Polish Enlightenment and their 9,431 books were processed. Parameters such as origin, places of birth and education, the geography of residence, migrations, and publications of the authors were analyzed in detail.
- Methodological value. Orekhov emphasized that, despite the local character of the material and the use of purely statistical methods, Aleksandrowska's study became one of the first examples of comprehensive mapping of the literary process. It made it possible to demonstrate clearly how cultural centers and provinces are formed, as well as to reveal the influence of political and social factors (for example, secularization or the partitions of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth) on literary geography.
- Contemporary context. The speaker drew parallels with current digital projects in the field of geopoetics and computational literary studies, including his own work on geocoding the Russian literary space of the 19th century. It was noted that this "forgotten episode" of Polish scholarship remains a model of careful work with data and can serve as a guide for contemporary researchers, despite the apparent "unproductiveness" of directly transferring such methods to Russian material (as the speaker himself wittily remarked).

The discussion that followed the talk touched on a wide range of questions: from the specifics of the administrative-territorial division of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth to general problems of visualizing historical and cultural data and the limits of the applicability of quantitative methods in the humanities.
