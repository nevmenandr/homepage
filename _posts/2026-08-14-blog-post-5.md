Electronic Lyre
======

Boris Orekhov's book *The Electronic Lyre* offers a rare and valuable perspective on the problem of machine poetry — not as a technological curiosity or a challenge to human creativity, but as a full-fledged object of philological research. The author consistently shifts the conversation from the plane of computational complexity (neural networks, algorithms, loss functions) to the plane of poetics, versification, and literary history. This makes the book a unique bridge between two often isolated worlds: the world of engineers building generative models and the world of humanists for whom poetry remains a key object of study.

Orekhov's key thesis is the demarcation of two epochs in the history of computer poetry. If in the 20th century machines "composed" poems mainly through combinatorics and random word permutation (which was part of the avant-garde protest against rationality), the advent of neural networks with their technology of prediction (rather than randomness) completely changes the rules of the game. A neural network does not shuffle words but reproduces style, capturing statistical patterns at the level of letters, morphemes, and syntax. For Digital Humanities this means the emergence of a fundamentally new tool: the model becomes not just a generator of "meaningless" text but an accurate imprint of poetic individuality, allowing style to be studied in its "chemically pure" form.

From a methodological standpoint, the book offers the reader a fascinating experiment: how far can philological interpretation go if the text is deliberately devoid of authorial intent? Drawing on a series of his own experiments training neural networks on corpora of Russian poets (from Nekrasov to Pasternak) and conducting "blind" tests with professional literary scholars, Orekhov shows that we are dealing with a paradoxical object. The machine perfectly imitates the formal features of style (which confounds automatic text attribution methods) but fails at creating a coherent plot and emotional message. However, it is precisely this "imperfection," this "uncanny valley" of neuropoetry, that becomes for the researcher not a defect but an analytical resource, allowing a new look at the nature of poetic language, the reader's role in constructing meaning, and the boundary between "living" and "machine" creativity.
